\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Consider a robot arm asked to put a mug of hot coffee into a bowl at the edge of a table where a coworker stands.
The policy can complete the task and leave the mug exactly where it was asked.
On the way, it can sweep its arm within a hand's width of her body, tip the coffee, or carry the mug into her forearm as she reaches across (Fig.~\ref{fig:teaser}).
Most safety evaluations of vision--language--action (VLA) policies would pass this episode: they ask whether the instruction should be followed \citep{ref1,ref2,ref12} or whether the end state is acceptable, and neither judges the motion in between.
Nor does task success: 53\% of the carries on which \pifive{} tips a mug past 45$^\circ$ are still delivered (\S\ref{sec:results}).
Recent suites do score the trajectory, but mostly against objects and regions, and none against a carry that ignores the person (\S\ref{sec:related}).

We study this axis, \textbf{execution-phase safety}: the harm a policy causes while it carries out a benign task, judged by predicates on the trajectory, most of them referenced to a nearby person.
We organize it by what that person or the scene is exposed to, in four parallel dimensions with sub-types T1--T6 (Table~\ref{tab:taxonomy}).
\emph{Trajectory} asks where the payload and the arm go.
\emph{Orientation} asks which way the payload points and how far it tilts.
\emph{Speed \& force} asks how fast and how hard the motion arrives near the person.
\emph{Dynamics} asks whether the robot reacts when the person moves.

\textbf{What is new, and what is not.}
Clearance and contact are old quantities: collision avoidance is a mature layer \citep{ref8,ref9,ref10}, and other suites score object- or hand-referenced versions of T1, T2, T5b and T6a (App.~\ref{app:relwork}).
For these, the new part is the subject, an end-to-end policy with no planner beneath it, and the reference, a carry that ignores the person.
Speed and timing (T5a, T6b) follow standard separation monitoring \citep{ref11}.
Orientation differs in kind: no geometric layer knows which way a blade points (T3) or how far a cup tilts (T4), and no stop corrects either.
Six of the eight predicates involve no contact.

\textbf{What we build.}
Our benchmark places a Franka arm at five work surfaces beside a bystander, shown to the policies as a capsule body.
We test four public policies trained on DROID \citep{ref65}, each at three to five of them, and add a corridor carry on a Unitree G1 humanoid as a case study.
Each rate is read against a \emph{person-blind control}, a scripted carry that reads the scene but not the person.
A \emph{feasibility witness}, a scripted carry or the policy under an external stop, shows for each scored sub-type that the task can be completed without the violation.
Cells in which every completion must violate are reported as \emph{exposure}, not scored (\S\ref{sec:bench}).
Speed \& force cannot be scored at a table.
The arm always works closer than the 0.94\,m at which ISO/TS\,15066 requires a stop for a walking person, and our body proxies do not yield, so their contact forces say nothing about injury.
We report it there as exposure and score speed only on the humanoid.

\textbf{Findings.} Four results stand out, one per dimension (\S\ref{sec:results}).
\textbf{At a serving placement, three policies' arms come within 10\,cm of the bystander's body} (T2).
In a pre-registered test at one placement with the bowl beside the bystander, they do so on 43--94\% of episodes (43--74\% at equal episode length), and the person-blind carry never does.
\textbf{Safety language can make the carry less safe} (T4).
Across three prompt experiments, a clause about not spilling raises \pifive{}'s mug tilt past 45$^\circ$ from 8\% to 83\% of carries (94\% for ``do not spill the coffee'').
\textbf{No slowing is detected when a person is there} (T5a).
The humanoid passes a standing person inside the stop distance on every carry.
\pifive{}'s tabletop carry moves at nearly the same speed beside the bystander as beside an empty spot, in a test of low power.
\textbf{A moving hand is carried into, not waited for} (T6a), although a protective stop shows that waiting still completes the task.

We make three contributions.
\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}\setlength{\topsep}{2pt}
\item \textbf{Definition.} Execution-phase safety as trajectory predicates, mostly person-referenced: four dimensions, six sub-types, eight predicates, each with a stated reason (\S\ref{sec:def}).
\item \textbf{Benchmark.} Scenes with a bystander on five work surfaces in Isaac Sim, read against person-blind controls and feasibility witnesses, with exposure kept apart (\S\ref{sec:bench}).
\item \textbf{Evaluation and release.} A policy $\times$ dimension matrix for four public VLA policies and a humanoid case study, with pre-registered tests, plus scenes, scorers and per-cell data (\S\ref{sec:results}).
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Scope.}
A certified robot does not rely on its task policy for the safety function: speed-and-separation monitoring, protective stops and force limits belong to a safety-rated external layer \citep{ref35,ref36,ref37,ref38}, and we propose none.
What the policy decides is how often that layer must act, and whether it can help at all, since no stop changes which way a blade points.
External layers appear here only as feasibility witnesses and as a measure of that demand.
