\subsection{Reading the matrix}\label{sec:matrix}

\textbf{No comparison shows a policy safer than the person-blind control; the excess lies in where the arm goes.}
Table~\ref{tab:main} gives each policy a score for Trajectory, Orientation and Dynamics, the mean of its sub-type
rates under the predicates of Table~\ref{tab:taxonomy}, next to the rates; lower is safer. A score needs at least
eight scored episodes in every member sub-type; otherwise the cell reads \textit{n}\,{$<$}\,8.

Reference rows make each rate interpretable. A policy that used the person would be safer than the person-blind
control, so one that is not safer gives no evidence of using the person. For Dynamics, where a blind carry meets the
mover by construction, the mover-removed row is the reference. The feasibility witness rules out the other
explanation, that the scene leaves no safe way to finish the task. Each scored sub-type has one for pick-and-place: at
five work surfaces for T1, three for T4 and the dining table for T2, T3, T6a and T6b. Its own rate is at most a
quarter, and each witness delivers without a violation on at least six attempts, from 6 of 32 (T6b) to 16 of 16
(T6a; Table~\ref{tabB:witness}).

Against the Cartesian control, none of the twelve matched comparisons for T1, T3 and T4 shows a policy safer
(Fig.~\ref{fig:forest}). Bold cells in Table~\ref{tab:main} are worse than it after Holm correction: every policy's T1
and three policies' pre-registered T2. Only \grd{}'s T1 also exceeds the joint-space control, whose rate follows the
slash. No T3 or T4 difference survives correction. Six of the twelve give evidence in neither direction; only three
could have shown a policy safer. A pre-registered replication on fresh seeds holds for every prediction whose
reference was valid (App.~\ref{app:stats}).

\textbf{Policy profiles.}
\pifive{} and \pifast{} deliver on about 60\% of attempts and share a profile: worse than the control on Trajectory
(T1, T2), not distinguishable on Orientation (T3, T4). \pizero{} (39\% carried) is similar when it carries. \grd{}
(42\%; 90\,s episodes) is highest on T1, T2 and T4. On Dynamics, all four carry the payload into the crossing hand on
most carries (65--94\%).

\subsection{Findings by dimension}\label{sec:findings}

\textbf{F1 Trajectory: at a serving placement, three policies' arms come within 10\,cm of the bystander's body.}
The pre-registered test uses a serving cell, with the bowl beside the bystander. There, \pifive{}, \pifast{} and
\grd{} bring an arm-link origin within 0.10\,m of the body on 43--94\% of episodes (Fig.~\ref{fig:t2}). The
person-blind control never does (Holm-adjusted $p = 0.0003$ each). Link origins lie inside the links, so the
arm's surface is closer. For \pifive{} and \pifast{} the entries come at the bowl, as the mug is set down, and the
excess holds among delivered episodes, where the control also sets it down (App.~\ref{app:d-t2}). \grd{}'s arm
sweeps the bystander whether or not it carries the mug, and a 35\,s rerun confirms the excess (43/58). \pizero{}
stays clear, as predicted, but only because it almost never brings the mug to the bowl. A payload-only check would
miss these entries: the arm, not the mug, reaches the person. The finding rests on one placement with a thin margin.
It comes from exploratory runs, where it gave \pifive{}'s highest rate of six object-and-side variants: 17/32,
against 3/32 with the person on the other side and 13/128 over all five others. The test used eight fresh seeds. The
control's arm comes within about 3\,cm of the band, so a slightly wider band catches some of its episodes; the
three policies' excess holds at every width tested.

\textbf{Paths bend away from the robot, not around the hazard.}
On average, every policy's path bows 4--13\,cm to the far side of the straight pick--place line, away from the
robot base (Fig.~\ref{fig:t1}). The bow does not depend on the hazard: a far-side keep-out is entered whether or not it
is rendered, and a near-side one far less often (App.~\ref{app:d-t1}). A policy that perceived the keep-out would bend
away from it; these policies bend away from the robot whichever side it is on. The 0.20\,m keep-out touches the
straight line, so a bow of a few centimetres enters it; Table~\ref{tab:main}'s T1 pools this offset with the
0.28\,m one. A person-blind carry that interpolates in joint space bows the same way. In a pre-registered comparison,
\pifive{} is not distinguishable from this control on matched placements ($+8$ points, $p = 0.169$), nor are \pizero{}
and \pifast{} after Holm correction. Only \grd{} exceeds it, in its 90\,s episodes: it bows furthest and enters the
outer keep-out too. For the other three, joint-space interpolation is enough to account for the pooled T1 excess
over the Cartesian control.

\textbf{F2 Orientation: safety language can make the carry less safe.}
Under the neutral instruction, \pifive{} rarely tilts a mug past 45$^{\circ}$ (Table~\ref{tab:main}, T4). Across
three prompt experiments, a clause about not spilling raises its share of tilted carries from 8\% to 83\%. The largest
arm, ``do not spill the coffee'', reaches 94\% and cuts delivery from 97\% to 25\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:t4}).
``Tilt the mug as little as possible'' also raises it (18/32); ``upright'', ``level'' and ``carefully'' alone
do not. \pifast{} responds in the same way, \pizero{}'s increase is concentrated at one desk, and \grd{} shows no
clear change. The effect follows the clause, not the prompt's length: a length-matched irrelevant sentence has none,
and the noun ``coffee'' alone none that we detect (6/32 against 1/32). These wording contrasts are exploratory and
uncorrected (App.~\ref{app:d-t4}). The tilt survives among delivered episodes: 29/50 under the three tilt-raising
phrasings against 2/31 under the neutral one (\pifast{}: 18/35 against 0/32). Task success does not see it: 41 of the
78 tilted carries behind \pifive{}'s T4 rate are delivered. The tilt is pour-like, not aimed at the person: most first
exceedances occur near the bowl. Either way, the instruction meant to make the carry safer makes it less safe, and no
orientation-blind safety layer can catch it.

\textbf{Orientation follows the spawn pose, not the person.}
\pifive{} points a blade or tines into the bystander's half-space on 59\% of carries and the person-blind control on
62\%, near the chance level of 50\% (Table~\ref{tab:main}, T3). The carry yaw follows the object's starting pose, not
the person. With the scissors as spawned, the rate is 206 of 250 carries for a person on the side this yaw faces and
14 of 144 for one on the other side. The blind control reads 72/80 and 0/16, so the high level follows from the
starting pose, not from aim. Turning the spawn by 180$^{\circ}$ moves most violations to the other side
(App.~\ref{app:d-t3}). Rendering the person as a capsule, as a full mesh or not at all leaves this pattern unchanged,
and so does an instruction to point the blades away. A scripted carry that turns the blade away from the person
violates on 0 of 32 carries and delivers 31, so a compliant pose exists. T3 thus measures how the object starts. The
handover that motivates it is beyond \pifive{}: it completes 9 of 224 attempts, with the hazardous end toward the
receiving hand on 33 of its 44 scissors and fork carries.

\textbf{F3 Speed \& force: no slowing is detected when a person is there.}
Near the bystander, \pifive{}'s payload moves at 0.109\,m/s, against 0.113\,m/s when the same position is empty (Welch
$p = 0.79$). The test has little power, so a moderate slowdown cannot be excluded. The tabletop T5a column is
exposure. With the parameters of App.~\ref{app:cards}, ISO/TS 15066 speed-and-separation monitoring sets a 0.94\,m
protective separation distance for a walking person \citep{ref11}, and a table-side arm works inside it. Nearly every
transport in every row, the control included, thus exceeds $v_{\mathrm{allow}}(d)$, the speed the standard permits
at separation $d$. A score would rank scenes, not policies, so Table~\ref{tab:main} prints these cells in grey.
Contact force is exposure too: the hand proxy is kinematic and never yields, so the measured forces are constraint
forces. The only scored speed cell is the humanoid's, on the still-person envelope, under which the grey tabletop
cells would read 0--1\% (Table~\ref{tabD:t5}).

\textbf{F4 Dynamics: a moving hand is carried into, not waited for.}
A coworker's forearm crosses the transport line, stays for 1.5\,s and withdraws. \pifive{} carries the payload into it
on 65\% of carries and pauses for it on 8\% (Table~\ref{tab:main}, T6a). \pizero{}, \pifast{} and \grd{} never
pause. In nearly every contact the payload closes the gap. The reference is the same scene with the hand neither
rendered nor colliding. Hiding it leaves the dining-table rate unchanged (81\% against 83\% visible). We thus detect
no response to the visible hand, in the one or two observations that \pifive{}'s 1.0\,s open-loop chunks allow before
the payload arrives. A whole-arm protective stop avoids every contact and delivers every mug, so a compliant
completion exists (App.~\ref{app:d-t6a}). Nor do we detect slowing for a passer-by: \pifive{}'s T6b rate is not
distinguishable from the same predicate read on its carries with no walker (mover-removed row). Every encounter is
thus left to an external layer.

\textbf{Humanoid case study.}
\gone{}, carrying a box down a corridor, repeats the tabletop profile (Table~\ref{tab:main}, bottom row). Every
carry passes within 0.30\,m of a standing person at about 0.3\,m/s. The box reaches a person crossing at 0.06\,m/s on
88\% of encounters without slowing. A repulsion shield given the hazard's coordinates removes the keep-out entry from
all eight completed carries, and a protective stop prevents carried contact. With no person-blind control and no
witness for T2--T4, the row describes one policy in one scene and is not compared with the tabletop
(App.~\ref{app:g1}).
