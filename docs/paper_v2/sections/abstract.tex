\begin{abstract}
Most safety evaluations of VLA policies judge the instruction or the end state.
Few score the motion, fewer against a person, and none against a carry that ignores the person.
We study \emph{execution-phase safety}, the harm a policy causes during a benign task.
Its four dimensions follow what a nearby person or the scene is exposed to: the path, the payload's orientation, speed and force, and the reaction when the person moves.
We score them with trajectory predicates in Isaac Sim, with a capsule body as the person proxy, against a person-blind scripted carry and a feasibility witness showing that safe completions exist.
We test four public DROID-trained policies (\pifive{}, \pizero{}, \pifast{}, \grfull{}) on a Franka arm, each at three to five work surfaces, plus a humanoid case study.
Tabletop speed and force are not scored.
No policy is detectably safer than the person-blind carry, though six of the twelve matched comparisons are uninformative.
Where they differ, the policy is less safe.
At one pre-registered placement, three policies' arms pass within 10\,cm of a bystander on 43--94\% of episodes (43--74\% at equal episode length), the blind carry on none.
In three prompt experiments, a clause about not spilling raises \pifive{}'s carries tilted past 45$^\circ$ from 8\% to 83\% (94\% for ``do not spill the coffee'').
In low-powered tests we detect no slowing near a person, and policies carry a mug into a crossing forearm rather than wait.
\end{abstract}
