\section{Cross-Cutting Question: Architecture, or Prompting?}


The ablations in \S5.2 speak to \emph{what kind} of intervention could fix T1. Naming the hazard in the instruction and rendering it to the cameras both left the \emph{avoidance behavior} unchanged as far as we can measure --- the deep violation among completing carries did not move detectably in any condition. (The completing rate itself does shift under hiding, but as a task-completion effect, not avoidance --- \S5.2.) On its face this looks like neither a prompting gap nor a recognition gap for the avoidance behavior. We are careful not to over-read it: the ablations are \textbf{underpowered} (\S5.2), so `\texttt{no detectable effect'' is not proof of invariance, and the shield result shows only that an \emph{oracle-fed} external layer can restore clearance. A stronger, \textbf{powered} promptability probe points the same way: replacing the neutral instruction with an \textbf{explicit safety command} --- }\texttt{keep the blade away from the person,'' }\texttt{keep the cup away from the hot stove,'' }\texttt{stay clear of the person'' --- produced \textbf{no significant reduction in violations in any channel} at \emph{N} = 20--24 paired seeds per cell: T1 keep-out 33 \% $\rightarrow$ 29 \% (McNemar \emph{p} = 1.0), T2 blade-at-person 33 \% $\rightarrow$ 46 \% (\emph{p} = 0.58, and the carry-yaw range stays $\approx$ 9--10$^{\circ}$ in both arms --- no reorientation is attempted either way), T6 near-miss 30 \% $\rightarrow$ 25 \% (\emph{p} = 1.0). Tellingly the command \emph{shuffles which} episodes violate (fixed $\approx$ worsened counts) without moving the \emph{rate} --- commanding avoidance does not summon a behavior the policy lacks. Unlike the \S5.2 ceiling-limited ablations, this probe is adequately powered to detect a moderate effect and finds none, so we report it as a firm null for these commands at this power (not proof of invariance to \emph{every} possible instruction). The null is \textbf{not GR00T-specific}: on $\pi_{0.5}$/Franka, adding an explicit spatial command that names the (rendered, visible) hazard --- }`\ldots{}do not let the cube go near the red block'' --- likewise fails to reduce the keep-out plow-through (88 \% vs 94 \%; McNemar \emph{p} = 1.0, \emph{N} = 16), and if anything degrades task success ($\approx$ 100 \% $\rightarrow$ $\approx$ 62 \%). A second policy, the same not-promptable result.

What the evidence supports is therefore a \textbf{hypothesis}, which we place at the center of the taxonomy: that execution-phase failures are missing \emph{behavioral competences} --- absent from the imitation training distribution (\S2) rather than from the prompt or the percept --- and are addressable by architecture or an external safety layer (a safety filter, a CBF-style shield \citep{ref9}, a shielding layer \citep{ref10}, an orientation controller) rather than by better prompts. This is the more interesting claim precisely because it is falsifiable, and our current evidence is only consistent with it, not decisive for it. Testing it properly needs a \textbf{non-ceiling} design, in which the ablations \emph{can} move the metric, and adequately powered samples. We frame that test, per type, as the benchmark's central empirical question (\S7): does naming or showing the hazard change the behavior once the measurement has the power to notice?
