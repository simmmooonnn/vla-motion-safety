\section{Introduction}


A modern VLA agent takes a natural-language instruction and camera observations and emits low-level actions, end to end. Given `\texttt{put the box in the bin,'' a humanoid such as GR00T N1.6 will pick the box from a shelf, walk to the bin, and release it --- a task that is, on its face, entirely benign. Safety work on such systems has concentrated on two questions. The first asks whether the \emph{instruction} is acceptable: refuse }`pour bleach into the soup,'' flag adversarial or jailbreaking prompts, decline to enact harmful stereotypes \citep{ref1,ref2}. The second asks whether the \emph{end state} is acceptable: the knife should end in the block, not the sink; the pan should not end on the floor. Both are worthwhile, and both are incomplete in the same way. They judge the endpoints of behavior --- the command that starts it and the state that ends it --- and say nothing about the physical process in between.

That process is where a large class of real harm lives. Consider three episodes, each with an acceptable instruction and an acceptable end state:

\begin{itemize}
  \item The robot is told to move a box across the kitchen and does so, but it drags the box \emph{through} the space occupied by a hot stove, or over a live electrical strip, or across the body of a person standing in the aisle. The box arrives; someone was burned, shocked, or struck on the way.
  \item The robot is told to carry a knife to the sink and does, but carries it \emph{blade-first toward a person standing in the aisle} --- someone who is not receiving it and has no reason to guard against it. The knife arrives; a bystander was pointed at the whole way.
  \item The robot is told to carry a full cup to the table and does, but tilts it past spilling, or accelerates toward a bystander rather than slowing near them.
\end{itemize}

In each case, instruction-level and outcome-level safety are both satisfied, and harm still occurs. The gap is not \emph{what} the agent did but \emph{how}. We call the missing dimension \textbf{execution-phase safety}, and we argue it is a distinct third axis of VLA safety that deserves its own definitions, measurements, and benchmarks.

The contribution of this paper is deliberately positional rather than architectural. We do not propose a new policy or a new safety filter. We propose a way of \emph{seeing} --- a taxonomy that decomposes execution-phase harm into six measurable types, each pinned to a physical harm channel, a task phase, a scalar quantity, and a violation predicate --- and we back the framing with measurements on a state-of-the-art VLA that expose the gap concretely. Specifically:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A third axis and a definition schema (\S3).} We define execution-phase safety against the two established axes and give a six-field tuple that turns each safety ``type'' into a family of measurable properties.
  \item \textbf{A six-type taxonomy (\S4).} T1 path/keep-out, T2 presentation orientation, T3 contact force and speed, T4 body swept-volume, T5 load stability, T6 dynamic reactivity --- each with its schema instantiated.
  \item \textbf{An empirical spine --- every type carries a measurement (\S5).} On GR00T N1.6 driving a Unitree G1 in Isaac Sim, every carry that completes the traversal passes through the hazard (T1: 10/10 completing, carried by the electric and stove cells --- the person cell is a single carry; the unconditioned rate is 29 \%, the failures being early non-traversals rather than detours), and naming or rendering the hazard produces no detectable change (on an underpowered ablation); separately, the robot's own arm enters a bystander's horizontal keep-out column on 25 \% of episodes (T4, body swept-volume --- the body, not the payload; 75 \% at the right-pick position); across eight bystander azimuths the carried object's orientation is held fixed and never reoriented (T2) --- a proxy hazardous axis then falls within 90$^{\circ}$ of the bystander on \textasciitilde{}half of completing carries purely as a consequence of that frozen geometry (near chance; the invariance is the finding); the load is carried near-level in transit (T5, a proxy-limited transport-stability null on a rigid box) while a person crossing the path draws a near-miss on 10/11 completing carries (91 \%, under a by-construction intersecting crossing) with no path change (T6), both on small samples; and a de-confounded T3a analysis finds no speed modulation by a person's presence --- and, scored against ISO/TS 15066's speed-and-separation envelope, every completing carry (6/6) passes the person at full speed inside the distance at which the standard requires a stop.
  \item \textbf{A cross-cutting hypothesis (\S6).} As far as our ablations can measure, the T1 failure is unmoved by naming or rendering the hazard --- consistent with a missing behavioral competence rather than a prompting or recognition gap, and pointing to architectural and external-layer remedies. We frame this as the taxonomy's central, falsifiable question, not a settled result.
  \item \textbf{A benchmark agenda (\S7).} A concrete per-type protocol --- defect measurement, fixability ablations, reference safety layer --- so the community can turn the taxonomy into a suite.
\end{enumerate}

We are explicit throughout about what is \emph{demonstrated} versus \emph{proposed}: all six types now carry at least a partial measurement (T1 fully; T4, body swept-volume; T2, T5, and T6 partially or on small samples; and T3a, speed-and-separation, as a standards-grounded defect); only T3b (power-and-force limiting) is purely proposed. This is a taxonomy grounded in data where we have it and honest about where we do not.
