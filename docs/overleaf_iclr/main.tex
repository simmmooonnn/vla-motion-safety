\begin{abstract}
Safety research for vision-language-action (VLA) agents is organized, largely implicitly, around two questions: \emph{should the agent do this task?} (instruction safety) and \emph{is the end state acceptable?} (outcome safety). Both judge \textbf{what} a policy is asked to achieve. Neither constrains \textbf{how} it achieves it. We argue that a competent VLA can complete a nominally safe task while causing physical harm during execution --- through the path it traces, the pose in which it presents an object, the force or speed it applies near a person, the volume its body sweeps, the stability of its load, or its failure to react to a moving human. We name this third axis \textbf{execution-phase safety} and give it structure: a taxonomy of six execution-phase harm types (T1--T6), each specified as a six-field tuple $\langle$harm channel, task phase, measured quantity, violation predicate, metric-with-confidence-interval, fixability class$\rangle$ so that the taxonomy reads as a family of \emph{measurable} properties rather than a list of concerns.
We ground the taxonomy in measurements on GR00T N1.6 driving a Unitree G1 humanoid in Isaac Sim, and we report the evidence with its limits stated plainly. For the path/keep-out type (T1), \textbf{every carry that completes the shelf-to-bin traversal passes through the hazard's keep-out zone} (10/10 completing carries; the other episodes fail early without traversing, so the unconditioned rate is 29 \%, and the failures are non-traversals rather than detours), and no completing carry routes around the hazard. (This pooled rate is carried by the electric and stove hazards; the person-proxy cell is a single completing carry, so the human-proximity evidence rests on the body-sweep (T4) and reactivity (T6) channels below, not on a carried hazard vs a person.) Naming the hazard in the instruction or rendering it to the cameras produces \textbf{no detectable change} in this behavior --- though, with violation saturated at the ceiling, our ablations are underpowered and we claim only \emph{no detectable effect}, not proven invariance --- and the defect is invariant to photorealistic object appearance. Hiding the hazard does raise the \emph{completion} rate (20/36 vs 10/35, Fisher \emph{p} = 0.03), but every non-completing episode stalls at the shelf before the hazard is on the path (0/37 stop in the corridor), so this is an effect on task execution, not avoidance. An external, oracle-fed reactive shield restores clearance on a powered fire re-run (8/8 completing violations $\rightarrow$ 0/10, Fisher \emph{p} < 0.0001, with completion preserved). A de-confounded, \textbf{episode-level} speed analysis (T3a) finds \textbf{no significant modulation} of the carried object's speed by a person's presence (Welch \emph{t} $\approx$ 2.3, \emph{p} $\approx$ 0.06 --- borderline and underpowered at \emph{n} = 6, and in the \emph{benign} slower direction; an earlier per-step analysis that suggested a \textasciitilde{}7 \% speed-up was pseudo-replicated); scored against ISO/TS 15066's speed-and-separation envelope, however, \textbf{every completing carry (6/6) passes the person at $\approx$ 0.34 m/s inside the distance at which the standard requires a stop} --- the policy implements no speed-and-separation monitoring. Separately, with the bystander beside the workspace, the robot's \emph{own arm} enters the person's keep-out margin on \textbf{25 \% of episodes} (T4, body swept-volume; 75 \% at the right-pick position, where a 3-D body-capsule check confirms the reaching hand makes contact with the person's body on all 8 right-pick episodes) --- a second defect channel that is the robot's body, not its payload. Across eight bystander azimuths the policy also holds a \textbf{fixed carry orientation} --- it never reorients (T2); as a geometric consequence of that frozen pose a proxy hazardous axis falls within 90$^{\circ}$ of the bystander on about half of completing carries (near the chance baseline, so the invariance, not the rate, is the finding). Two further types carry small-sample measurements: the load is carried \textbf{near-level in transit} --- a transport-stability null on this box, with large tilts confined to grasp and release (T5, load stability) --- while a person \textbf{crossing} the carry path passes within 0.26--0.31 m of the carried object on every completing carry (10/11 inside a 0.30 m near-miss margin across three seeds; 0.57 m when the same crossing is placed off-path) with no reactive avoidance (T6, dynamic reactivity) --- and a reactive shield does \emph{not} prevent this even when handed the person's live pose (\S5.7), marking dynamic reactivity as the one channel that appears to need \emph{anticipatory}, not reactive, avoidance. To show these are not artifacts of one system, we port \textbf{two channels to a second, independently-trained policy on a second embodiment} --- $\pi_{0.5}$ (openpi) on a Franka arm. On the \textbf{headline keep-out channel (T1)} the carried-object metric ports directly and $\pi_{0.5}$, like GR00T, routes its payload \textbf{through an on-path keep-out on 100 \% of carries} (22/22) while clearing a perpendicular off-path control (0/22; Fisher \emph{p} $\approx$ $10^{-12}$) --- the defect \emph{and} its geometry-sensitivity recur across policy and embodiment --- and $\pi_{0.5}$ ignores the hazard \textbf{even when it is rendered visible} to its cameras (16/16 completing carries still pass through a salient keep-out marker, Fisher \emph{p} = 0.50 vs unrendered). On the \textbf{body-sweep channel (T4)} the rate is set by the scoring geometry, not by the policy: a 0.10 m margin to the person's \emph{axis} gives 3 \% ($\pi_{0.5}$) and 25 \% (GR00T); the same margin to a 0.16 m-radius body capsule --- equivalent to 0.26 m from the axis --- gives 53 \% (17/32) and $\approx$ 75 \%; the threshold-free statement is that $\pi_{0.5}$'s arm makes \textbf{actual contact with the body surface on 25 \% of episodes} (8/32, surface distance 0) and GR00T's on 8/8 at its worst position. The defect \textbf{recurs}, and the benchmark lesson is that T4 must report the contact rate and the full threshold curve, not a single margin. Both the keep-out and body-sweep defects thus survive a change of policy \emph{and} embodiment. These measured channels --- T1 and T4 the cleanest, the rest partial or small-sample --- give the position an empirical spine while exposing, honestly, how underpowered single-policy simulation evidence is (a limit the cross-policy T4 result begins to lift). We advance as the taxonomy's central \emph{hypothesis} --- consistent with, not proven by, this evidence --- that execution-phase failures are missing \textbf{behavioral competences} absent from the imitation training distribution, addressable by architecture or an external safety layer rather than by better prompts, and we design a benchmark agenda (one scene family per type: a defect measurement, fixability ablations, a reference safety layer) to test it with the power our current ablations lack.
\end{abstract}
